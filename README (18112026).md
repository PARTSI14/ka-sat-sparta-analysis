# KA-SAT (Viasat) 2022 Attack: SPARTA & MITRE ATT&CK Mapping

> Portfolio project analysing a real-world space-sector cyber incident with the SPARTA and MITRE ATT&CK frameworks.

## 1. Executive summary

On 24 February 2022, around the time Russia's invasion of Ukraine began, an attacker disrupted Viasat's KA-SAT consumer satellite broadband service.
Several thousand customers in Ukraine and tens of thousands of fixed-broadband customers across Europe lost service. The spillover also cut remote monitoring of about 5,800 Enercon wind turbines in Germany.
The attacker got in through a misconfigured VPN appliance, moved into the trusted management segment, and used legitimate management commands to push the AcidRain wiper to SurfBeam2/2+ modems, which overwrote their flash memory.
The satellite itself was never touched. The attacker turned the provider's own ground management plane against the user terminals that trusted it.
Key lesson: in a satellite network the ground and management segment is the real attack surface. Remote access, privileged mass-commanding and firmware integrity need the same protection as the space segment.

| # | Phase | SPARTA technique | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Initial access via VPN | IA-0007 Compromise Ground System | Viasat: attacker exploited a misconfiguration in a VPN appliance to gain remote access to the trusted management segment of the KA-SAT network |

| 3 | Legitimate-looking commands | IA-0007.02 Malicious Commanding via Valid GS (by analogy) | Viasat: legitimate, targeted management commands sent to many modems simultaneously |

## 5. Detection opportunities (SOC view)

| Phase | What a SOC could have seen |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Initial access via VPN | VPN logins from new source IPs/ASNs or geolocations, at unusual hours, or for accounts that rarely use remote access; configuration drift on the VPN appliance compared with a hardened baseline; repeated authentication failures followed by a success |
| 2. Lateral movement | First-time connections from the VPN address pool into the management segment; admin accounts used from unusual hosts; new remote sessions (SSH/RDP/management API) to the modem-management servers |
| 3. Mass management commands | A volume anomaly: commands to thousands of modems in minutes with no matching change ticket or maintenance window; a file pushed to terminals whose hash does not match any approved firmware or package |
| 4. User terminals targeted | Unusual command types sent to customer equipment (file execution or writes, not routine configuration); commands targeting whole regions at once |
| 5. AcidRain wiper | Mass simultaneous modem reboots followed by modems that never re-register; a sharp drop in active AAA/network sessions; loss of terminal telemetry |
| 6. DoS from modems | Spike in DHCP and other traffic from authenticated terminals in one region (Ukraine) on a single network partition; network-capacity alarms correlated with the management-plane activity above |

## 7. Open questions & limitations

- No full public forensic report from Viasat: it publishes no indicators, logs or full access timeline, and has not described the exact VPN misconfiguration.
- **Delivery mechanism disputed in framing:** SentinelLabs called the incident a supply-chain style compromise of the KA-SAT management mechanism. Viasat confirmed AcidRain was run on the modems through a legitimate management command but pushed back on parts of that framing. This analysis follows Viasat's description (management commands, T1072).
- **DoS vs. wiper relationship unclear:** public sources do not say whether the modems that generated DoS traffic were the same ones later wiped, or how the DoS traffic was triggered.
- **Attribution out of scope:** the EU, UK and US publicly attributed the attack to Russia in May 2022, and SentinelLabs found medium-confidence code overlap with VPNFilter's `dstr` plugin. This analysis maps behaviour, not actors.
- **SPARTA coverage gap:** IA-0007 (Compromise Ground System) describes ground-segment compromise as a path to the spacecraft. In KA-SAT the initial access matches (VPN → management network), but the satellite itself was never touched; the target was the user terminals. The mapping is therefore by analogy, which is why this analysis also uses MITRE ATT&CK.
- **SPARTA Lateral Movement scope:** SPARTA defines this tactic as movement within a spacecraft or between nodes in a constellation (ST0007). Movement inside a ground network is out of scope, so LM-0007 is applied by analogy and ATT&CK T1021 covers the actual behaviour.
- **DE-0004 Masquerading considered and rejected:** the technique describes forging or imitating an authorized origin. In KA-SAT the commands came from the real (compromised) management system, so only the effect matches, not the method. IA-0007.02 covers this case better.
- **Reverse trust direction:** SPARTA IA-0009.03 describes a compromised user terminal as a springboard *into* the provider and spacecraft. KA-SAT shows the opposite path: a compromised provider management plane used *against* thousands of user terminals that trusted it. This provider-to-customer direction is not explicitly modelled in SPARTA; ATT&CK T1199 Trusted Relationship covers it.
- **Dwell time unknown:** Viasat has not disclosed when the attacker first gained access. A pre-positioned foothold, held until 24 Feb, is plausible but unconfirmed.
- **Why destroy instead of persist? (assessment):** the timing suggests the objective was disruption of communications at the start of the invasion, not intelligence collection. A wiper makes the outage last (modems had to be replaced or reflashed) and destroys forensic evidence, at the cost of "burning" the access.
